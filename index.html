Who Wrote the Fundamental Rights

The provenance of Part III of the Constitution of India, 1895–1950

Public memory of the Indian Constitution runs through a single name. The documentary record runs through dozens. This study traces every phrase of Articles 14 to 32 backward from the words adopted on 26 November 1949, through the Draft Constitution of February 1948, the Constitutional Adviser's draft of October 1947, the Advisory Committee and Fundamental Rights Sub-Committee reports of April 1947, and the members' own drafts of March 1947, to the minuted act that put each phrase there.

Two questions are asked of each right. Who was the pioneer: which Indian constitutional document first stated the rule, or documented a clear intent to secure it? And who was the framer: whose proposal produced the surviving words? These are lines of credit, not a ranking. Every attribution carries a primary citation, and where the record is silent, the charts say so.

What the record shows

13

of the nineteen rights were framed or co-framed by B. N. Rau, the Constitutional Adviser, on evidence recovered from the appendices to the Sub-Committee's daily minutes

12

of the nineteen pioneer cells belong to the two Nehrus: Motilal's 1928 report and Jawaharlal's 1931 Karachi resolution, six rows each

8

are held or shared by K. M. Munshi; Article 14's "equal protection of the laws" is his March 1947 draft, verbatim

3

rights bear the documented hand of B. R. Ambedkar, chairman of the Drafting Committee: he drove the decision that abolished untouchability (Article 17), gave Article 30 its surviving form, and moved Article 22 in its entirety, the only right with no constitutional pioneer

On the evidence

Sources. B. Shiva Rao (ed.), The Framing of India's Constitution: Select Documents, vols. II–IV, for the member drafts of March 1947, the committee minutes and reports of 1947, the Constitutional Adviser's draft of October 1947, the Draft Constitution of February 1948 and the Drafting Committee's letter of 3 November 1949; together with the Constituent Assembly Debates for the floor amendments of 1948–49, and the antecedent documents digitised by the Centre for Law and Policy Research.

What is not published here. The working corpus (verbatim transcriptions and OCR of the source volumes, the sitting PDFs, and the project's working papers) is held offline rather than republished, since it is substantially third-party text. Documents in this repository cite it by filename; those files are available on request.

Corrections. This account was rebuilt once already, after a second check found that a widely repeated attribution for the property article collapsed against the Official Report. The corrected table has since been checked twice more. Corrections supported by a primary source are welcome.
